# Consistency Building — Protocols for Inconsistent Learners

## Understanding Inconsistency
The Inconsistency pattern: strong start → momentum builds → skip one day → guilt → extended break → restart with overambitious plan → repeat cycle.

The core problem is not motivation — it's the response to breaking the streak. The first break is normal. The catastrophizing response to the break ("I've ruined everything") causes the extended absence.

## The "Never Miss Twice" Rule
James Clear: "Missing once is an accident. Missing twice is the start of a new habit."

The rule eliminates the binary "did I maintain the streak or not?" with a more resilient "did I miss twice in a row?"

Implementation in curriculum:
- Every task has a "minimum viable version": if full task is impossible, what's the 10-minute version?
- The minimum viable version prevents total breaks: doing something tiny maintains the chain
- Never let perfect be the enemy of good

Recovery protocol (for when a break happens):
- Do NOT restart with a harder or longer version ("I'll do double tomorrow")
- Restart with a version that is 50% of the normal version (reducing re-entry friction)
- Acknowledge the restart without judgment: "Day 1 again. That's fine."

## Sprint-Based Structure
Three-day micro-sprints reduce the psychological weight of long commitments.

Sprint structure:
- Days 1-3: active practice (scaled to availability)
- Day 4: "catch-up day" — do any missed practice from days 1-3, or rest if days 1-3 were complete
- Days 5-7: new sprint begins

Advantages of sprint structure:
- "Only 3 days until the next catch-up day" is psychologically easier than "I have to do this every day forever"
- The catch-up day removes the streak-breaking anxiety — missing day 2 is recoverable by day 4
- Week boundaries create natural review and restart opportunities

## Environmental Triggers for Consistency
Consistency is easier when the behavior is triggered by an environmental cue rather than a decision:

Same time: the behavior happens at the same time every day → the time itself becomes the trigger
Same place: using the same location for the behavior → the place itself becomes the trigger
Same sequence: the behavior always follows the same preceding action → the preceding action becomes the trigger

The goal is to make the behavior automatic: triggered by environment, not requiring active decision.

For inconsistent learners: one strong trigger is more important than the right time or place.

## Progress Visibility
Research (Amabile and Kramer): the single strongest motivator is perceived progress. When people see they are making progress, they feel better and work harder.

Progress visibility tools:
- Visual streak calendar (X on each day) — don't break the chain
- Weekly review summary: "This week I completed 4 of 7 sessions. That's better than last week's 2."
- Milestone celebration: every 7 days completed is worth acknowledging

For inconsistent learners: celebrate 5 of 7 days, not 7 of 7. 5 of 7 is 71% consistency and produces real results. Demanding 100% creates the conditions for failure and abandonment.

## Reducing Session Startup Friction
The highest-risk moment for inconsistent learners is the transition from "not doing" to "doing."

Friction reduction tactics:
- Prepare the session environment the night before
- Have a "launch sequence": same 3 actions that precede every session (e.g., make tea, open app, start timer)
- Commitment devices: sign up for a class, pay for a session in advance, tell someone
- Body doubling: work alongside another person (real or virtual via Focusmate) — accountability without reporting

## Building Commitment Through Small Promises
Research: people who make small, public commitments are more likely to follow through (Cialdini, commitment and consistency principle).

Application:
- "I will practice for 15 minutes after dinner" (said aloud or in writing)
- Telling one person your plan for today (accountability partner, coach, app)
- End-of-session preview: "Tomorrow I will [specific action] at [specific time]"

The commitment becomes part of identity: "I am someone who keeps small promises to myself."

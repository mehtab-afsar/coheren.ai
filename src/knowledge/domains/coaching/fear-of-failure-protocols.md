# Fear of Failure — Coaching Protocols and Interventions

## Understanding Fear of Failure
Fear of failure (atychiphobia in clinical contexts) is characterized by: avoiding tasks where performance matters, preference for easy tasks where success is guaranteed, catastrophizing about potential failure outcomes, interpreting failure as evidence of personal inadequacy.

The cognitive distortion: equating performance outcome with self-worth. "If I fail at this → I am a failure as a person."

## Reframing Failure as Information
The most evidence-backed intervention: change the meaning of failure.

Protocol:
1. "What is the worst realistic outcome if I try and fail?"
2. "What would I learn from that outcome?"
3. "What is the worst realistic outcome if I don't try?"
4. In almost all cases, trying and failing produces more learning and costs less than not trying

Scientist framing: "I am running an experiment, not making a prediction. Experiments don't fail — they produce data."
- Success data: "This approach works"
- Failure data: "This approach doesn't work — I now know something I didn't know before"

## Graduated Exposure
The clinical treatment for anxiety-based avoidance: systematic, voluntary, graduated exposure to feared situations.

Hierarchy example for someone who fears being evaluated:
1. Write something private that no one sees (level 1 — lowest threat)
2. Share it with one trusted person (level 2)
3. Post it anonymously online (level 3)
4. Post it with your name on a small platform (level 4)
5. Submit to a publication (level 5)

Each level must be held until anxiety reduces before moving up. Rushing the hierarchy maintains anxiety; going through it reduces it.

In curriculum design: never put the high-exposure activity in Phase 1. Private practice before public performance.

## Process vs. Outcome Goals
Outcome goals ("win the race," "get an A," "be selected") are largely outside your control — they depend on others, randomness, and circumstances. Process goals ("run 4 training sessions this week," "study 2 hours per day," "submit the application") are entirely within your control.

Redirect focus from outcomes to process:
- "Did I complete my planned practice?" (Yes/No — binary, achievable)
- "Did I give full effort?" (Yes/No — independent of others)
- Outcome goals create fear of failure; process goals create success from effort

## Self-Worth Independence from Performance
The core work: separating "I failed at this task" from "I am a failure."
Carol Dweck research: people with fixed mindset ("talent is fixed") fear failure because it reveals their ability. People with growth mindset ("ability grows with effort") see failure as feedback about current strategy.

Fixed mindset statement: "I'm just not smart enough for this."
Growth mindset reframe: "I haven't learned this yet. What would help me learn it?"

Practice: after any failure, complete the sentence: "I learned _____ from this experience, which means next time I will _____."

## The Experiment Frame in Curriculum Design
Every task for fear-of-failure users should be framed as:
- "Experiment" not "performance"
- "Practice session" not "assessment"
- "Draft" not "final version"
- "Try it and see what happens" not "do it correctly"

Success criteria must be observation-based, not quality-based:
- Bad: "Do 10 push-ups with correct form"
- Good: "Attempt 10 push-ups and notice what feels hard — there's no wrong answer"
- The goal is to complete the reps, not to perform them well (performance comes later)

## When to Progress Out of "Experiment" Framing
The experiment frame is a scaffold, not a permanent identity. Remove it when:
- The user has completed 10+ sessions without catastrophic failure
- The user reports reduced anxiety before starting tasks
- The user is asking for more challenge (sign of restored confidence)

Then gradually introduce:
- Tasks with visible output ("complete and save the draft" → "share the draft with one person")
- Slightly higher stakes, one level at a time
- Never jump multiple levels of exposure in one phase

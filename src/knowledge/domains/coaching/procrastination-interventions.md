# Procrastination Interventions — Evidence-Based Protocols

## Understanding Procrastination
Procrastination is an emotion regulation problem, not a time management problem. People procrastinate to avoid the negative emotions associated with a task (boredom, anxiety, frustration, self-doubt) — not because they lack willpower.

This reframe changes the intervention: the goal is to reduce the aversive emotional response associated with starting, not to increase willpower.

## Implementation Intentions (Strongest Evidence)
Research (Gollwitzer): Implementation intentions of the form "When [situation], I will [behavior]" increase follow-through by 200-300% compared to "I will do X."

Format: "When [specific trigger], I will [specific action] for [specific duration]."
- Bad: "I will study tomorrow"
- Good: "When I sit down at my desk at 9 AM with coffee, I will open my notes app and write one sentence for 2 minutes"

The "when" must be specific: a time, a location, or an existing routine trigger. The action must be small enough that motivation is not required.

## The Two-Minute Starter Step
Origin: From procrastination research and the Two-Minute Rule (Clear).
The brain's "starting resistance" disappears once you begin. The hardest moment is the first moment.

Rule: Design every task to start with a step that takes under 2 minutes and requires zero setup.
- "Write a full report" → Starter: "Open the document and type the heading"
- "Go to the gym" → Starter: "Put on gym clothes"
- "Practice guitar" → Starter: "Pick up the guitar and sit with it"

Once the starter step is done, the brain often continues without needing additional activation. If it doesn't — just the starter step counts as a win for that day.

## Temptation Bundling
Research (Milkman): Pairing a habit you need to do with something you enjoy increases completion rate.
- Only watch your favorite TV show while on the treadmill
- Only listen to your favorite podcast while doing meal prep
- Only drink your favorite coffee while working on the hardest task

The enjoyable activity becomes a reward that is only available during the productive behavior.

## The 5-Minute Focus Session
For severe procrastination: commit to only 5 minutes. Not 25 (Pomodoro). 5 minutes.
- "I will work on this for exactly 5 minutes, then I can stop"
- After 5 minutes, most people continue (Zeigarnik effect: unfinished tasks create cognitive tension that pulls you toward completion)
- If they stop after 5 minutes: they still made progress — celebrate this

## Reducing Activation Energy
Every barrier between you and starting reduces the likelihood of starting.
- Preparation: lay out everything you need the night before
- Remove choices: "Tuesday and Thursday at 7 PM I practice, always, no decision required"
- Remove competing behaviors: log out of social media, put phone in another room, use a focus app
- The 20-second rule: make the productive behavior 20 seconds easier to start

## Self-Compassion After Procrastination
Research (Neff, Sirois): Self-criticism after procrastinating makes future procrastination more likely. Self-compassion makes future procrastination less likely.
- "I procrastinated. That was hard for a reason. I'm human. What's one small step I can take right now?"
- Self-criticism activates threat response → more avoidance
- Self-compassion activates growth mindset → approach behavior

## Task Redesign for Procrastinators
When a task is consistently avoided, the task itself is the problem — not the person.
- Too vague: "Work on project" → "Write the introduction paragraph only"
- Too large: break it into pieces where each piece is completable in one session
- Too aversive: identify what specifically feels aversive and address that element
- Too boring: pair it with something enjoyable (temptation bundling)

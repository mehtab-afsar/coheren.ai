# Music Learning — Instrument or Production from Beginner to Competent

## Phase 1: Foundational Technique (Weeks 1–6)
Music skill is stored in motor memory (cerebellum and basal ganglia). Motor memory is built through slow, correct repetition — not fast, sloppy repetition. The speed at which you can play something correctly is the only speed you should practice at initially.

Practice rule: Practice slowly enough to play correctly every time. Speed comes automatically after correct patterns are deeply encoded. Playing something wrong 50 times encodes the wrong pattern — then you must unlearn it.

Guitar specifics:
- Calluses: fingers will hurt for 2-3 weeks. Practice 15-20 min daily. Rest for 5-10 min. Pushing through to 60 min in week 1 causes injury.
- Open chords first: Em, Am, E, A, D, G, C (in order of difficulty for beginners)
- Chord transition drill: set a 60 bpm metronome, change chords exactly on the beat — slower is fine, in-time is essential
- Day 1: Em and Am only. Strum pattern: down-down-down-down at 50 bpm. 15 minutes max.

Piano specifics:
- Hand position: curved fingers, wrists level, elbows slightly below keyboard level
- Start hands separately: learn right hand melody, then left hand accompaniment, then combine very slowly
- Note reading vs. ear: choose one primary method first (reading is better for classical, ear is better for jazz/pop)
- Week 1: learn C major scale, both hands, correctly. Then basic C-F-G chord shapes.

Week 1–2: Single concept focus
- One chord (guitar) or one scale (piano) only. Play it 100 times per day correctly.
- Milestone: Play the week's concept without looking at your hands for 16 bars at 60 bpm

Week 3–4: Chord transitions or scale patterns
- Guitar: transition between 2 chords smoothly — Em to Am, 100 times per day
- Piano: C major scale with correct fingering, both hands, ascending and descending
- Add metronome to everything — rhythm is as important as notes
- Milestone: 2-chord transition at 80 bpm without hesitation

Week 5–6: First song
- Choose a song with maximum 3 chords or a simple melody
- Learn one section at a time (verse, chorus) — not the whole song at once
- Guitar: "Horse With No Name" (Em, D6) or "Wonderwall" (Em7, G, Dsus4, A7sus4)
- Piano: "Let It Be" chord progression or "Für Elise" (first 16 bars)
- Milestone: One complete song from start to finish, with correct rhythm, at slow tempo

## Phase 2: Expanding Repertoire and Music Theory (Weeks 7–16)
Songs become the vehicle for technique — you learn technique in the context of music you care about.

Practice session structure:
- 5 min: warm up (scales, chord transitions, known material)
- 15 min: technique focus (specific challenge in current song)
- 15 min: learning new material
- 5 min: play something you can already play well (ends on confidence)

Music theory integration:
- Month 2: Major and minor keys, the major scale, intervals
- Month 3: Chord construction (major, minor, dominant 7th)
- Month 4: The I-IV-V-I progression (this underlies 60% of Western pop music)
- Application: every theory concept learned → find it in a song you already play

Song selection strategy:
- Every new song should teach you one new technique: barre chord, fingerpicking pattern, syncopation, etc.
- Don't learn 10 songs at 50% — learn 3 songs at 100%
- Record yourself: your ears calibrate to what you think you sound like, not what you actually sound like

Milestone: Play 5 complete songs from memory at full tempo with correct rhythm.

## Phase 3: Performance and Musicality (Weeks 17–24)
Technical competence and musical expression are different. This phase develops feel, timing, and the ability to make music that affects listeners.

Playing with others:
- Jam sessions force you to listen and adapt — most important skill development after year 1
- Find a jam partner at similar level: playing with beginners who know different things teaches different skills
- Recording yourself playing: the most efficient practice tool — you hear things you miss while playing

Ear training:
- Identify intervals by ear: every interval has a famous song (major third = "When the Saints Go Marching In")
- Transcribe one bar of a song by ear per week — listen, play, check, adjust
- This builds the ability to learn songs without tabs/sheet music

Dynamics and expression:
- Play the same phrase at different volumes — this is where music becomes communication
- Pause and silence are notes too: practice rests as deliberately as you practice notes
- Learn the difference between playing correctly and playing musically

Milestone: Play one complete performance piece from memory in front of another person, maintained from beginning to end.

## Common Pitfalls
- Practicing too fast: encoding wrong patterns that become the thing to unlearn later
- Skipping metronome: good time feel is what separates competent from musical players
- Only learning songs, ignoring technique: scales and exercises aren't optional
- Never recording: impossible to accurately self-assess without audio reference

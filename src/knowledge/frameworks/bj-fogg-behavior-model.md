# BJ Fogg Behavior Model — B=MAP

## The Core Formula
Behavior = Motivation × Ability × Prompt. All three elements must converge at the same moment for a behavior to occur. If any one of the three is absent, the behavior will not happen. This is not a metaphor — it is a mechanistic description of how human behavior works at the neural level.

The most common design mistake is targeting Motivation first. Motivation is the least controllable variable. The right leverage points are Ability (make it easier) and Prompt (make it reliable).

## Motivation — The Unreliable Variable
Motivation operates along three dimensions: sensation (pleasure/pain), anticipation (hope/fear), and belonging (acceptance/rejection). All three are inherently unstable — they fluctuate with sleep quality, stress, mood, time of day, and social context.

High motivation is a temporary state, not a personality trait. Designing habits that require high motivation will produce inconsistent behavior. The Fogg model predicts: when motivation drops below the Action Line for a given difficulty level, the behavior will not occur. This is not weakness — it is physics.

The implication: motivation is a useful amplifier but a terrible foundation. Use it to do more when it is high; do not depend on it to do anything when it is low.

## Ability — The Controllable Variable
Ability is the ease with which a person can perform a behavior right now. Five factors determine ability: time required, money required, physical effort, mental effort, and routine disruption. Reducing any of these factors increases ability.

The Ability axis is the highest-leverage design target because increases in ability are durable — they do not fluctuate with mood. A frictionless habit fires even on bad days.

Practical ability increases:
- Reduce time to 2 minutes or less (starter step)
- Place the cue physically adjacent to the trigger (shoes by the bed, book on the pillow)
- Remove decision-making from the habit execution (predetermined sequences, scripts)
- Front-load the hard part: the hardest moment is starting, not continuing

## Prompt — The Missing Piece
A Prompt is a cue that tells the brain to execute a behavior now. Without a prompt, no behavior occurs regardless of motivation or ability. This is why good intentions fail: people have motivation and ability but no reliable trigger.

Three types of Prompt:
1. Person Prompt — internal, relies on remembering. Least reliable. Effective only for high-frequency behaviors.
2. Context Prompt — environmental, requires setting up the environment. Medium reliability. Effective when space is under your control.
3. Action Prompt — anchored to another behavior (Tiny Habit recipe). Most reliable. The anchor behavior IS the reminder.

The Tiny Habit recipe encodes the most reliable prompt type: "After I [existing behavior], I will [new behavior]." The anchor must be a behavior that already happens reliably and consistently.

## The Action Line
The Action Line is the threshold above which a behavior will occur. It sits at a specific combination of motivation and ability. A difficult behavior requires high motivation to cross the Action Line. An easy behavior requires almost no motivation.

The practical insight: shrink the behavior until it sits below the Action Line even at your lowest motivation state. The starter step should be so small that refusal would feel ridiculous.

## Pearl Habits — Identity-Level Anchors
The most powerful Tiny Habits are anchored to behaviors that are tied to identity. "After I make coffee, I will do two push-ups" is more durable than "After I sit at my desk, I will exercise" because coffee-making carries stronger identity attachment for most people.

Ask: what behavior do you do automatically, every day, without thinking? Build new habits on top of those.

## Application to Stone Types
- FearOfFailure: Shrink the behavior until public failure is impossible. The tiny version builds the proof of capability that fear of failure cannot argue with.
- Inconsistency: Remove Person Prompts entirely. Inconsistency is often a prompt design problem, not a motivation problem.
- TimeConstraint: Reduce ability barrier first. A 2-minute version eliminates the time objection.
- LowConfidence: Design the first version to be unambiguously achievable. The first three wins build more confidence than any encouragement.
- Overcommitment: Apply the Action Line insight inversely — the habit is too hard, not too ambitious. Shrink it.

## Common Design Errors
1. Setting the behavior too large at the start (overestimating motivation persistence)
2. Choosing unreliable prompts (person prompts, calendar reminders, willpower)
3. Skipping celebration (emotion is what wires the habit, not repetition alone)
4. Growing the habit too fast before the anchor is solid
5. Treating motivation as character rather than fluctuating state

Source: Fogg, B.J. (2019). Tiny Habits: The Small Changes That Change Everything. Fogg Behavior Model research, Stanford Persuasive Tech Lab.

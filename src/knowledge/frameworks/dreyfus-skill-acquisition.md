# Dreyfus Model of Skill Acquisition

## Overview
Stuart and Hubert Dreyfus (1980) proposed a five-stage model of skill acquisition based on their study of chess players, airline pilots, and military personnel. The model describes how humans move from rule-following novices to intuitive experts, and predicts what type of instruction is effective at each stage.

The Dreyfus model is the empirical foundation for phase-based curriculum design. Different phases are not arbitrary divisions of time — they correspond to qualitatively different cognitive states that require different instructional approaches.

Source: Dreyfus, S.E. & Dreyfus, H.L. (1980). A Five-Stage Model of the Mental Activities Involved in Directed Skill Acquisition. University of California at Berkeley Operations Research Center. Dreyfus, H.L. & Dreyfus, S.E. (1986). Mind Over Machine: The Power of Human Intuition and Expertise in the Era of the Computer.

## The Five Stages

### Stage 1: Novice
Behavior: Rule-following, context-free. The novice applies explicit, pre-defined rules without understanding the situational context in which rules should bend. No discretionary judgment.

Characteristics:
- Needs clear, unambiguous instructions with defined features to recognize
- Cannot distinguish relevant from irrelevant aspects of the situation
- Performance is brittle — any deviation from learned scenarios causes confusion
- High cognitive load from rule-following itself
- Errors are non-discriminative (doesn't know which error matters more)

Instructional implication: Provide clear step-by-step rules. Reduce ambiguity to zero. Do not ask for judgment calls. Success criteria must be binary and observable. Volume of repetitions is more important than variety at this stage.

Curriculum phase: Foundation, early weeks.

### Stage 2: Advanced Beginner
Behavior: Rule-following with situational recognition. The learner starts to recognize recurring patterns and develops "aspects" — guidelines that apply in specific situations, unlike the universal rules of Stage 1.

Characteristics:
- Recognizes recurring patterns from prior experience
- Applies situational guidelines but cannot yet prioritize between them
- Still low emotional investment — treats all aspects as equally important
- Performance is more robust but still fragile in novel situations
- Begins to recognize when a rule doesn't fit without knowing what to do instead

Instructional implication: Introduce real-world examples that show rules failing. Provide case studies. Allow limited judgment calls with scaffolded reflection. Feedback on pattern recognition, not just execution.

Curriculum phase: Foundation, later weeks. Transition to Development.

### Stage 3: Competent
Behavior: Goal-directed, hierarchical planning. The competent performer chooses an organizing plan and selects which aspects of the situation are relevant to that plan. For the first time, the person emotionally invests in outcomes.

Characteristics:
- Can formulate and execute a deliberate plan
- Feels genuine satisfaction from success and distress from failure (emotional investment)
- Decision-making is slower than expert but more reliable than beginner
- Performance is slower due to deliberate planning but more robust
- Can adapt to novel situations by modifying the plan

Instructional implication: Present problems that require planning. Encourage the learner to articulate their plan before execution. Reflection after failure is particularly valuable here. Emotional engagement (real stakes) is appropriate.

Curriculum phase: Development phase. Core of the learning journey.

### Stage 4: Proficient
Behavior: Intuitive situational recognition, deliberate decision-making. The proficient performer sees the situation holistically and knows immediately what type of response is required — but still consciously deliberates on the specific action.

Characteristics:
- Recognizes situations immediately and holistically rather than by feature decomposition
- Deviation from the normal situation stands out against background — rapid anomaly detection
- Decision-making remains deliberate even when perception is intuitive
- Performance is smooth when situation matches experience, effortful when it doesn't
- Teaching ability emerges — can articulate what they see, not just what they do

Instructional implication: Expose to wide variety of situations to build pattern library. Case studies with complex tradeoffs. Reflection on perception ("what made you see it as X?"). Peer discussion more valuable than more instruction.

Curriculum phase: Late Development, early Mastery.

### Stage 5: Expert
Behavior: Intuitive situational recognition AND intuitive response selection. The expert does not deliberate on either what to do or how to do it — the response flows directly from the situation perception. Rules are irrelevant and even counterproductive.

Characteristics:
- Performance is effortless and fluid — "in the zone"
- Cannot easily explain decisions (tacit knowledge) — expertise is largely non-verbalized
- Disrupted by being asked to follow rules (rule-following interferes with expert intuition)
- Able to handle true novelty by analogical reasoning from similar situations
- Highly attuned to subtle cues that novices are completely unaware of

Instructional implication: Expert learning is primarily peer consultation, novel challenges, and deliberate practice at the performance boundary. Instruction in the traditional sense is counterproductive. Challenge at the edge of current capability.

Curriculum phase: Mastery phase and beyond.

## Phase Split Rationale — Dreyfus in Numbers
Based on the Dreyfus model and Bloom's Mastery Learning research, the optimal curriculum phase splits are:
- Foundation (Novice → Advanced Beginner): ~30-35% of total timeline
- Development (Competent → Proficient): ~40-45% of total timeline
- Mastery (Proficient → Expert edge): ~20-30% of total timeline

These ratios hold across domains with modest adjustment for:
- Highly structured domains (programming, language): Foundation can compress to 25%
- Psychomotor domains (sports, instruments): Foundation often extends to 40%
- Abstract domains (leadership, discipline): Development may extend to 50%

## Dreyfus and Stone-Based Adjustments

**SkillGap (High severity)**: User is below Novice in the domain — explicit prerequisite knowledge is missing. Extend Foundation by +20%. Add explicit prerequisite modules. Do not advance to rule-flexible content until prerequisite gaps are filled.

**FearOfFailure**: The emotional investment that arrives at Stage 3 (Competent) is the danger point for FearOfFailure users — real stakes produce real anxiety. Extend Foundation by 10% to build mastery security before emotional stakes rise.

**Overcommitment**: Overcommitted users tend to push toward Stage 4-5 behaviors (holistic pattern recognition) before Stage 3 competence is solid. The result is confident-feeling but brittle performance. Do not let them skip the deliberate planning phase.

**High self-efficacy (≥8) + prior domain experience**: User may already be at Stage 2-3. Compress Foundation by 15-20% and use a placement assessment to determine actual entry stage.

## Non-Linear Progression
Dreyfus progression is not strictly sequential or time-locked. A learner can move through Stage 1-2 quickly in a domain they have adjacent experience in, and stall for extended periods at Stage 3 (the competent-to-proficient transition is often the longest).

The stage is not determined by time spent — it is determined by the nature of the cognitive processing observed in performance. Graduation criteria must be behavioral, not temporal.

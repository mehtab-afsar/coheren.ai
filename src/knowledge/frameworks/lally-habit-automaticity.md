# Habit Automaticity — Lally et al., UCL 2010

## The Study and Its Findings
Phillippa Lally and colleagues at University College London conducted the first empirical study tracking the automaticity of new habits over time in everyday settings. 96 participants chose a new eating, drinking, or exercise behavior and tracked their daily performance and automaticity ratings for 12 weeks.

Key finding: Habit automaticity increased as a logistic (S-curve) function over time, not linearly. The average time to reach plateau automaticity was **66 days** (range: 18 to 254 days). The "21 days to a habit" claim has no empirical foundation — it was a misquoted self-report from a plastic surgeon's observation about patient adjustment time.

Source: Lally, P., van Jaarsveld, C.H.M., Potts, H.W.W., & Wardle, J. (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. European Journal of Social Psychology, 40(6), 998-1009.

## The S-Curve of Automaticity
Automaticity does not grow linearly with repetitions. It follows a sigmoid (S-shaped) curve:
- **Phase 1 (Days 1-21 approx.)**: Slow initial growth. Behavior requires full conscious attention and deliberate decision-making. Motivation is the primary driver. This is the highest-dropout risk phase.
- **Phase 2 (Days 22-66 approx.)**: Accelerating growth. Context associations strengthen. The behavior begins to feel "normal" though still requires some conscious initiation.
- **Phase 3 (Day 66+ to plateau)**: Asymptotic approach to automaticity ceiling. Behavior begins to trigger on context cues without deliberate decision.

The plateau level varies by behavior complexity. Simple behaviors (drinking a glass of water after breakfast) reach higher automaticity levels and reach them faster. Complex behaviors (50-minute gym sessions) reach lower plateau levels and take longer.

## The Missed Day Finding
One of the most important practical findings: missing a single day during acquisition did NOT significantly impair the long-term automaticity trajectory. The growth curve was robust to occasional misses.

Coaching implication: Catastrophizing about missed days is cognitively and motivationally costly — and factually wrong. One skip does not reset the habit formation process. The critical error is the "what-the-hell effect": one skip followed by quitting because "I've already ruined it." The miss itself is harmless; the interpretation of the miss is the problem.

## The Valley of Low Motivation (Days 10-21)
Lally's data is consistent with self-determination theory on this point: the period approximately 10-21 days into a new behavior is the phase with the highest dropout risk and the lowest reported motivation. The novelty effect has worn off, automaticity has not yet developed, and the behavior must be maintained by conscious effort alone.

This phase must be anticipated and planned for, not hoped away. Coaching strategies for the valley:
- Reduce behavior difficulty during this window (smallest possible version is acceptable)
- Increase external supports (accountability, reminders, social context)
- Explicitly name the valley: "This is the hardest part. It gets easier after week 3."
- Track streaks or completion without evaluating performance (the behavior IS the success)

## Behavior Complexity and Domain Effects
More complex behaviors take longer:
- Simple dietary additions (fruit with lunch): ~18-30 days to substantial automaticity
- Exercise behaviors (gym session): ~50-80 days on average
- Cognitive/study behaviors: ~30-66 days, highly variable
- Multi-step routines: often >100 days for full automaticity

This has direct implications for curriculum timeline promises. A 21-day "habit challenge" for exercise will fail to produce automaticity in ~90% of participants by design — the intervention ends before the automaticity curve reaches stable levels.

## What Automaticity Actually Means
Automaticity has four components measured by the Self-Report Habit Index (SRHI):
1. **Automaticity**: "I do it automatically" — the behavior initiates without deliberate decision
2. **Lack of awareness**: "I do it without thinking" — reduced conscious monitoring during execution
3. **Efficiency**: "I do it with little mental effort" — reduced cognitive load
4. **History**: "I have been doing it for a long time" — temporal embedding

Full automaticity requires all four. Many people achieve automaticity-1 (initiates automatically) without automaticity-2-3 (still requires cognitive resources during execution). This is partial automaticity — still valuable but fragile under stress.

## Habit Fragility at Phase Transitions
Even fully automatic habits can be disrupted by context change (moving, travel, illness, relationship changes). Research shows that habits formed over longer periods with more consistent context are more resilient to context disruption.

Vacation disruption study (Wood et al.): ~40% of people report successful habit maintenance during vacation; ~60% report disruption, with about half of those failing to fully recover the habit after returning home.

Curriculum implication: build explicit disruption recovery protocols into the maintenance phase. The question is not "will context disrupt?" but "when it does, what is the restart plan?"

## Application to Curriculum and Timeline Design
1. Never promise habit automaticity before day 45. Research average is 66 days.
2. Design Foundation phase to reach at least day 30 before significant complexity is added.
3. Plan explicitly for the valley of low motivation at days 10-21: reduce difficulty, increase support.
4. The 66-day average means: a 30-day plan will leave the habit in Phase 2 — improving, but not automatic.
5. For goals with a central habit component, the effective curriculum minimum is 90 days.
6. Habit automaticity estimates should be adjusted upward for: complex behaviors, high-stress lifestyles, TimeConstraint stones, Inconsistency stones.
7. Missed days are recoverable — the intervention design should treat them as normal, not catastrophic.

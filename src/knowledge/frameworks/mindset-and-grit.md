# Mindset & Grit

## Growth Mindset — Carol Dweck
Growth mindset: the belief that abilities and intelligence can be developed through effort, good strategies, and guidance.
Fixed mindset: the belief that abilities are innate and static — you either have it or you don't.
People with a growth mindset embrace challenges (as opportunities to grow), persist through setbacks (seeing them as part of the process), view effort as the path to mastery, learn from criticism, and find inspiration in others' success.
People with a fixed mindset avoid challenges (to protect their image of intelligence), give up quickly (effort means you're not naturally talented), view criticism as an attack, and see others' success as a threat.

## Praising Process, Not Ability
Research shows that praising effort and strategy ("You worked really hard on that") builds growth mindset, while praising ability ("You're so smart") builds fixed mindset.
Applied to adult coaching: celebrate the process of showing up, not just the outcome. "You practiced every day this week" is more growth-mindset-supporting than "You're a natural."
When users struggle, reframe: "This is hard, which means you're in the growth zone. Your brain is working hardest right now."

## Grit — Angela Duckworth
Grit is passion plus perseverance for long-term goals. Grit predicts achievement better than talent in virtually every domain studied.
The four components of grit: Interest (genuine fascination with what you do), Practice (deliberate improvement beyond your comfort zone), Purpose (belief that your work matters beyond yourself), Hope (the ability to persist despite setbacks, with the belief that tomorrow can be better than today).
Grit is not fixed — it can be developed through deliberate practice, cultivation of purpose, and building a "growth narrative" about one's past struggles.

## The Plateau of Latent Potential
Progress is rarely visible until it suddenly becomes very visible. Weeks of consistent effort produce no measurable result — then breakthrough. This is not failure; it is the Plateau of Latent Potential.
The temptation to quit is highest just before the breakthrough. Users who quit at weeks 3–4 of a habit are usually quitting right before the neural pathway would have solidified.
Frame for users: "The bamboo tree grows underground for five years before breaking the surface. Your progress is real even when it is invisible."

## Struggling and Self-Compassion
Self-compassion (treating oneself with the same kindness one would offer a struggling friend) leads to greater motivation and better habit adherence than self-criticism.
Beating yourself up after a missed day activates the threat-defense system, which reduces motivation and increases avoidance. Self-compassion activates the care-and-connect system, which restores motivation.
When users struggle: normalize the difficulty, validate the emotion, and redirect to the smallest possible next action. "This is hard. Missing days is normal. What is the one tiny thing you could do right now?"

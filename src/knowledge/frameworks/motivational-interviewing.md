# Motivational Interviewing — Miller & Rollnick

## What MI Is
Motivational Interviewing (MI) is a collaborative, goal-oriented communication style designed to strengthen a person's own motivation for and commitment to change. It works by eliciting and reinforcing the person's own arguments for change rather than imposing external pressure.

MI is not a technique for convincing people. It is a method for helping people convince themselves. The underlying insight: ambivalence is normal and expected at the onset of any change attempt. MI works with ambivalence rather than against it.

Evidence: In a meta-analysis of 72 clinical trials, MI showed significantly better outcomes than no treatment or traditional advice across health behavior change domains. A 2025 MI chatbot study achieved 98% adherence to MI standards, outperforming human counselors on most fidelity metrics.

## The Spirit of MI — Four Core Principles
1. Partnership: The coach and person work together as equals. The person is the expert on their own life.
2. Acceptance: Absolute worth of the person is affirmed unconditionally. Their perspective is valid even when not aligned with change.
3. Compassion: The coach acts in the person's best interest, not the system's agenda.
4. Evocation: The goal is to draw out the person's own motivation and values — not to install new ones.

Violating these principles (directing, arguing, pressing) triggers psychological reactance — the person defends the status quo MORE strongly when pushed.

## OARS — The Four Core Skills

### Open Questions
Questions that cannot be answered with yes/no. They invite reflection and personal elaboration.
- "Walk me through what a typical week looks like for you."
- "What makes this important to you right now?"
- "What would be different if you succeeded?"
- "What has made it hard to start?"
Avoid: "Do you exercise regularly?" (closed). Use: "What does physical activity look like in your life?" (open).

### Affirmations
Genuine acknowledgments of the person's strengths, efforts, and values. Not compliments — observations.
- "You've been really honest about what's gotten in the way before."
- "It takes real awareness to notice that pattern in yourself."
- "That's a thoughtful way to look at it."
Avoid generic praise ("Great!"). Use specific behavioral affirmations.

### Reflective Listening
Mirroring back what the person said to demonstrate understanding and invite elaboration. The most powerful MI skill.
Types of reflection:
- Simple reflection: Repeat or rephrase the core content.
- Amplified reflection: Exaggerate slightly to invite correction.
- Double-sided reflection: "On one hand... and on the other hand..."
- Feeling reflection: Name the emotion beneath the content.
Reflections signal understanding and keep the person talking, which increases change-talk ratio.

### Summaries
Periodic recaps that collect, link, and reinforce change-talk while acknowledging sustain-talk. Three types:
- Collecting summary: gathers what has been said so far
- Linking summary: connects current statement to earlier change-talk
- Transitional summary: signals a shift in the conversation direction

## Change Talk vs. Sustain Talk
Change talk: statements that favor change. Four categories (DARN-CAT):
- Desire: "I want to..."
- Ability: "I could..."
- Reasons: "It would help me..."
- Need: "I have to..."
- Commitment: "I will..."
- Activation: "I'm ready to..."
- Taking steps: "I've already started..."

Sustain talk: statements that favor the status quo.
- "I'm not sure I can..."
- "It's too hard right now..."
- "I've tried before and it didn't work..."

Research (Amrhein et al., 2003): The strength of commitment language (not desire or reasons) is the single strongest predictor of actual behavior change. When a person says "I will" vs. "I want to," outcomes diverge significantly.

Key principle: Elicit change talk, reinforce it with reflections and affirmations, and do not argue against sustain talk (arguing reinforces it via reactance).

## The Readiness Ruler
Two questions that map to the two primary motivational drivers:
- "On a scale of 0-10, how important is it to you to make this change?"
- "On a scale of 0-10, how confident are you that you could succeed if you decided to?"

Follow-up for any answer below 8: "Why did you say [X] and not a lower number?" This elicits change talk — the person articulates their own reasons for change.

The combination of Importance and Confidence creates a 2×2 motivational typology:
- High importance + High confidence → Action-ready. Move quickly to planning.
- High importance + Low confidence → Classic FearOfFailure. Address self-efficacy first.
- Low importance + High confidence → Contemplative. Explore values alignment.
- Low importance + Low confidence → Precontemplation. Motivational work needed before planning.

## The OARS Funnel (Diagnostic Interview Pattern)
For assessment purposes, OARS is deployed in a funnel structure:
1. Open question (broad, invites elaboration)
2. Reflective listening response (builds rapport, elicits more)
3. Open question (narrower, more specific)
4. Affirmation (validates what was shared)
5. Diagnostic close (specific question to confirm hypothesis)
6. Summary reflection (collects key change-talk, invites correction)

This structure extracts diagnostic information while building alliance — it does not feel like an interrogation.

## Application to Coaching Stone Profiling
- High hedgeDensity in answers ("I guess", "maybe", "sort of") = sustain talk dominance = low readiness
- Short minimal answers to emotional questions = topic avoidance = possible Perfectionism or FearOfFailure
- Elaborate complaints about scheduling ("I never have time") = TimeConstraint severity signal
- Passive voice clusters ("things just fall apart") = Inconsistency + external locus of control
- "I've tried before" without explanation = past failure pattern, probe for what changed

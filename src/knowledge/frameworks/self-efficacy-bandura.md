# Self-Efficacy Theory — Albert Bandura

## The Core Claim
Self-efficacy is the belief in one's capacity to execute the behaviors required to produce specific outcomes. It is the single strongest predictor of whether a person will initiate a behavior, persist through obstacles, and recover from setbacks — stronger than actual ability, intelligence, or past performance.

Self-efficacy is not global confidence. It is domain- and task-specific. A person can have high self-efficacy for public speaking and low self-efficacy for physical fitness simultaneously. Coaching must target the specific behavioral domain.

Meta-analytic evidence (Stajkovic & Luthans, 1998): Self-efficacy had a weighted average correlation of 0.38 with work-related performance outcomes across 114 studies — one of the highest predictors documented in behavioral science.

## The Four Sources of Self-Efficacy

### 1. Mastery Experiences (Strongest Source — ~60% of variance)
Direct personal success is the most powerful builder of self-efficacy. Each success raises the efficacy ceiling; each failure, if attributed internally, lowers it.

Critical nuance: Easy successes do not build robust efficacy. Successes achieved through moderate challenge and persistence build stronger, more resilient efficacy than outcomes that required no effort. This is the research basis for "desirable difficulty" in learning.

Coaching implication: Early sprints should be calibrated so that the person succeeds at slightly challenging versions of the target behavior — not trivially easy, not overwhelming. The stretch zone, not the panic zone.

### 2. Vicarious Experiences (Social Modeling — ~20% of variance)
Observing similar others succeed raises self-efficacy. The more similar the model to the observer, the stronger the effect. "If they can do it, I can do it."

The key word is similar: watching elite athletes does not raise self-efficacy for fitness beginners. Watching someone at the same starting point succeed is what matters.

Coaching implication: Social proof from people at a similar starting level is more motivating than aspirational examples. Show "someone like you" succeeding.

### 3. Verbal Persuasion (Encouragement — ~10% of variance)
Being told you are capable of something raises efficacy modestly. The source must be credible and specific to matter. Credible: the person respects the source. Specific: "You handled that well because..." not "You're great."

Important limitation: Persuasion raises efficacy less than mastery experiences, and can backfire if the person is subsequently challenged and fails — it raises expectations without providing actual capability.

### 4. Physiological and Emotional States (~10% of variance)
Physical arousal, stress, fatigue, and mood all affect efficacy appraisals. A person who feels anxious before a task interprets that arousal as evidence of low ability. A person who feels calm interprets the same physiological state as energized readiness.

Coaching implication: Pre-task ritual design matters. Helping users reinterpret arousal ("that nervousness is energy") can shift efficacy without any change in actual ability.

## Efficacy Beliefs vs. Outcome Expectations
Self-efficacy is about "Can I do this?" Outcome expectation is about "Will doing this produce the result I want?" Both must be positive for action. A person can believe they can write every day (high efficacy) but doubt that writing will lead to publication (low outcome expectation). The intervention targets differ: efficacy → capability building; outcome expectation → evidence for the value of the behavior.

## Efficacy, Goals, and Performance — The Feedback Loop
High self-efficacy → sets higher goals → applies more strategies when facing obstacles → persists longer → achieves more → higher efficacy (virtuous cycle)

Low self-efficacy → sets lower goals or avoids → gives up sooner when challenged → achieves less → lower efficacy (vicious cycle)

Breaking the vicious cycle requires manufactured mastery experiences — deliberately structured early wins that demonstrate capability. The win must be genuine; perceived success (without actual challenge overcome) does not reliably raise efficacy.

## The LowConfidence Stone — Efficacy-Based Treatment
Users with the LowConfidence stone have chronically low self-efficacy in the goal domain, usually from repeated past failures. The intervention sequence:
1. Validate past difficulty (the task was genuinely hard, not evidence of personal deficiency)
2. Introduce the tiniest possible version of the behavior (mastery experience, guaranteed)
3. Make the first 3-5 experiences unambiguously successful (build the efficacy foundation)
4. Attribute success explicitly to the person's effort and capability (not luck, not the system)
5. Progressively increase challenge only after genuine efficacy gains are visible

Do NOT increase challenge before efficacy is stable — this reactivates the vicious cycle.

## Self-Efficacy and Recovery from Setbacks
High-efficacy people attribute failure to insufficient effort or inappropriate strategy (modifiable) and increase effort and adjust strategy. Low-efficacy people attribute failure to personal deficiency (stable, unmodifiable) and decrease effort or quit.

Coaching during failure: always attribute to strategy or effort (controllable), never to ability (fixed). "That approach didn't work" is better than "That was too hard for where you are."

## Connection to Habit Automaticity
Bandura predicted, and subsequent research confirmed: high self-efficacy accelerates habit automaticity. People with high efficacy in a domain encode behaviors more readily because they approach the behavior with less anxiety, allowing cleaner neural consolidation. This is why efficacy-building early in Foundation phase directly speeds up the timeline to automaticity.

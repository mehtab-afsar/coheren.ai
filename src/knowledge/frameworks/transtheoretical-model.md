# Transtheoretical Model (TTM) — Prochaska & DiClemente

## Overview
The Transtheoretical Model describes behavior change as a process that unfolds through six discrete stages, not a single event. Each stage represents a different level of readiness, and the most effective intervention depends entirely on which stage the person is in.

The most common coaching error: treating all users as if they are in the Action stage. A person in Contemplation needs to resolve ambivalence before any practical planning helps. Offering a 90-day curriculum to someone in Precontemplation is like giving directions to someone who hasn't decided they want to go anywhere.

Meta-analysis (Prochaska & Velicer, 1997): Stage-matched interventions outperform stage-mismatched ones across all health behavior domains studied.

## The Six Stages

### Stage 1: Precontemplation
The person is not seriously considering change in the next 6 months. They may be unaware of the problem, demoralized by past failures, or resistant due to external pressure. They typically do not identify as someone who needs to change.

Indicators in onboarding:
- Goal was set because of external pressure (partner, doctor, employer)
- Importance score ≤ 4
- No prior attempts, or dismissal of past attempts as irrelevant
- Defensive or brief answers to questions about obstacles

Intervention: Raise awareness and explore values. Do NOT offer a plan. Premature action planning increases dropout. Use MI to explore pros/cons and connect to identity values.

### Stage 2: Contemplation
The person is aware a problem exists and is seriously thinking about change but has not committed to action. Ambivalence is the defining feature — they can see both sides. "I know I should, but..."

Indicators in onboarding:
- Importance 5-7, confidence below 6
- Can articulate reasons to change but also reasons not to
- Significant sustain-talk present alongside change-talk
- History of starting but stopping; framing of past attempts as failures

Intervention: Tip the decisional balance toward change. Elicit and reinforce change-talk. Explore the discrepancy between current behavior and stated values. Do NOT pressure into premature commitment.

Curriculum implication: Add a Phase 0 (7-14 days) before technical content. Focus on identity-level motivation activation, values clarification, and extremely small starter behaviors. Goal is to get to Preparation stage before Foundation begins.

### Stage 3: Preparation
The person intends to take action soon (within 30 days) and has taken some small steps. They may have a rough plan but lack the structured approach. They are ready to receive practical guidance.

Indicators in onboarding:
- Importance ≥ 7, confidence 5-7
- Has tried before with partial success
- Can name specific obstacles
- Asking "how" questions rather than "whether" questions

Intervention: Help develop a concrete, specific action plan. Strengthen commitment. Address anticipated barriers. This is where curriculum planning becomes appropriate.

Curriculum implication: Standard Foundation phase is appropriate. Normal phase splits apply.

### Stage 4: Action
The person is actively working on behavior change. They have made observable modifications in the last 6 months. This stage requires the most commitment and energy.

Indicators in onboarding:
- Already started the goal activity
- Asking about optimization, not initiation
- High both importance and confidence (both ≥ 7)
- Minimal sustain-talk

Intervention: Reinforce progress, build self-efficacy, anticipate and plan for relapse triggers. The behavior is not yet automatic — maintenance support is critical.

Curriculum implication: Compress or skip Foundation. Begin in Development phase. Habit automaticity not yet reached regardless of confidence.

### Stage 5: Maintenance
The person has sustained behavior change for more than 6 months. The primary challenge is preventing relapse during high-stress or disruption periods.

Indicators: Goal is continuation/deepening rather than initiation. Person has established routines but identifies specific risk periods.

Intervention: Relapse prevention planning. Environmental design for disruption resilience. Identity consolidation ("I am someone who...").

### Stage 6: Termination (Rare)
The behavior has become fully automatic. No temptation, no conscious effort. The former problem behavior has zero appeal. For most health behaviors, this stage is theoretical rather than achievable — maintenance is the realistic long-term goal.

## Decisional Balance
People progress through stages when the perceived pros of changing outweigh the perceived cons. Research finding: pros and cons follow a predictable crossing pattern across stages.
- Precontemplation: Cons significantly outweigh pros
- Contemplation: Pros and cons roughly equal (ambivalence)
- Preparation: Pros begin to outweigh cons
- Action: Pros significantly outweigh cons

Coaching implication: Do not argue against cons (reactance). Help the person articulate their own pros. The ratio shifts through insight, not persuasion.

## Processes of Change
Ten processes are active at different stage transitions:
- Consciousness raising (Precontemplation → Contemplation)
- Emotional arousal / dramatic relief (Precontemplation → Contemplation)
- Self-reevaluation (Contemplation → Preparation)
- Social liberation (Contemplation → Preparation)
- Self-liberation / commitment (Preparation → Action)
- Reinforcement management (Action → Maintenance)
- Stimulus control (Action → Maintenance)
- Counterconditioning (Action → Maintenance)
- Helping relationships (all stages)
- Environmental reevaluation (Contemplation → Preparation)

## Stage Detection Heuristics for Onboarding
```
Precontemplation:  importance < 5 OR external pressure as sole motivation
Contemplation:     importance 5-7, ambivalent answers, significant sustain-talk
Preparation:       importance ≥ 7, self-efficacy 5-7, has a plan or prior attempts
Action:            already started, asking about optimization
Maintenance:       ongoing practice, asking about resilience and relapse prevention
```

## Relapse — Not a Stage, But a Reality
Prochaska found most people cycle through the stages 3-7 times before stable maintenance. Relapse is not failure — it is information. The average successful quitter relapses multiple times before permanent change. Curriculum design must account for this: Phase 1 should include explicit relapse-recovery protocols, not just habits.

Source: Prochaska, J.O. & DiClemente, C.C. (1983). Stages and processes of self-change of smoking. Prochaska, J.O. & Velicer, W.F. (1997). The Transtheoretical Model of Health Behavior Change. American Journal of Health Promotion.

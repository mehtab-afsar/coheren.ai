# Habit Formation and Context-Dependence — Wendy Wood

## The Central Discovery
Habits are not stored as behavior sequences — they are stored as context-behavior associations. The brain encodes: "In context X, perform behavior Y." This changes everything about how habits are built, maintained, and broken.

The critical implication: motivation and intention are largely irrelevant to habitual behavior once the habit is installed. The context fires the behavior automatically, bypassing deliberate decision-making. This is a feature (no willpower required) and a bug (context changes disrupt the habit regardless of motivation).

Source: Wood, W. & Neal, D.T. (2007). A new look at habits and the habit-goal interface. Psychological Review. Wood, W., Tam, L., & Witt, M.G. (2005). Changing circumstances, disrupting habits. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.

## Habit Architecture — How Context Associations Form
During habit acquisition, the hippocampus encodes the context-behavior pair: time, location, preceding behaviors, emotional state, social context, and internal cues. Over repetitions, this encoding transfers to the basal ganglia, which handles automatic execution without hippocampal involvement.

The transfer to basal ganglia is what produces automaticity — the feeling that the behavior "just happens." At this point, deliberate intention is no longer the primary driver. Context becomes the driver.

Automaticity indicators: the behavior feels effortless, you can do it while thinking about something else, skipping it creates mild discomfort, and it occurs even when you explicitly try not to do it (habit intrusion).

## The Life Transition Effect — Context as Leverage
Major life transitions (moving to a new city, starting a new job, having a first child, ending a relationship) disrupt established habit contexts. This makes transitions both a risk for existing habits and a unique opportunity for new ones.

Wood's research on student relocation found that people who moved cities were significantly more likely to successfully change health-related behaviors than those who stayed. The existing contextual cues that reinforced old habits simply disappeared.

Coaching implication: When a user is undergoing a major transition, this is the highest-leverage moment for habit installation. New environments mean no competing habit associations — the behavioral slate is temporarily clean.

Counter-implication: Users who are NOT in transition must actively reconstruct the contextual architecture. This is harder and requires explicit environment design.

## Environment Design — Context Engineering
Since habits are context-behavior associations, changing the context is the most powerful way to change the behavior. There are three strategies:

### 1. Disruption (breaking bad habits)
Remove or alter the context cues that fire the unwanted behavior. Friction is the mechanism:
- Remove the smartphone from the bedroom to break the phone-before-sleep habit
- Take a different commute route to break the fast-food stop habit
- Store high-calorie food in opaque containers at the back of shelves

Even small physical friction (moving something 2 feet further away) reduces behavior frequency significantly. One study found that office workers ate 46% fewer chocolates when the candy dish was moved from the desk to a drawer.

### 2. Construction (building new habits)
Place context cues directly adjacent to the target behavior trigger:
- Running shoes by the bed (cue for morning run)
- Water bottle on the desk (cue for hydration)
- Guitar in the living room, not in a case in the closet (reduces friction, increases cue salience)
- Meditation app as the phone lock screen (cue triggered by phone-checking behavior)

The research principle: visible, salient cues in the relevant context are the most reliable prompts.

### 3. Habit Stacking — Anchor Chaining
Attach new behaviors to existing strong habit contexts. The "after I" protocol is environment design: you are using an existing strong context-behavior association as the cue for the new behavior.

Critical selection criteria for anchor behaviors: they must be daily, reliable, and contextually similar to the target behavior. "After I brush my teeth" works better as an anchor for personal hygiene habits than for exercise habits because the contexts differ.

## Context Consistency — The 66-Day Evidence
Wood's meta-analysis is consistent with Lally et al. (2010): habits require consistent context-behavior pairing over many repetitions before automaticity emerges. The key word is consistent — same context, same behavior, same sequence.

Variable context (doing the behavior at different times, in different places, with different preceding behaviors) slows automaticity acquisition because the brain cannot form a stable association. Each variation is encoded as a slightly different context-behavior pair.

Coaching implication: the first 30 days of a new habit should prioritize consistency over performance. Same time, same place, same sequence, every day. Variation comes after automaticity.

## Stable vs. Unstable Contexts — Habit Durability
Habits formed in stable environments are more durable under stress. When cognitive resources are depleted (tired, stressed, overwhelmed), people rely more heavily on habitual behavior — but only habits formed in the current context fire reliably.

This explains why vacation disrupts habits: the context cues that fire the behavior at home do not exist in the hotel. The behavior must be consciously initiated, which fails when cognitive load is high.

Design principle for high-stress resilience: build habits with multiple overlapping context cues (time + location + preceding behavior), so that even if one cue is absent, others can trigger the behavior.

## Application to Stone Types
- TimeConstraint: context-dependency is an ally. A habit that always fires "after I do X" requires no scheduling. The anchor behavior IS the time allocation.
- Inconsistency: the core problem is often unstable context — behavior attempted at different times and places, making context-behavior association impossible. Fix the context before anything else.
- Overcommitment: overcommitted users fragment their habit contexts across too many domains. Consolidation (fewer habits, more consistent contexts) is more effective than diversification.
- FearOfFailure: private contexts (habits performed alone, in familiar settings) are the right starting environment. Public contexts raise arousal and disrupt automaticity acquisition in early stages.
